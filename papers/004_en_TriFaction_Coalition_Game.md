# Paper 4: Tri-Faction Coalition Game — Judicial, IT, and Mechanical

**Core game**: three factions contesting veto power, agenda-setting power, and the monopoly on violence
**Tools**: coalition games, veto-player theory, agenda setting, coup conditions
**Show anchor**: Judicial's violence, IT's information control, Mechanical's infrastructure
**Core thesis**: silo stability rests on the three factions' mutual checks; any two-faction alliance breaks the equilibrium
**Falsifiable corollary**: when Mechanical and IT ally, the administration is at its most vulnerable

---

## Abstract

The stability of a closed system is often misread as a binary "administration vs. residents" opposition. But *Silo* reveals a finer structure: the administration itself is not monolithic — it is composed of three factions, **Judicial, IT, and Mechanical**, each monopolizing one of three powers: violence, information, and infrastructure. This paper models their interaction as a coalition game and argues: the silo's long-run stability is essentially a **fragile equilibrium of three-way mutual checks** — and any two-faction alliance instantly breaks it. The most dangerous, and most likely, alliance is the one between the infrastructure-holding Mechanical and the information-holding IT.

## 1. The Scene: Three Distrustful Power Centers

Inside the silo, power is divided:

- **Judicial**: holds violence, maintains order, carries out the "cleaning";
- **IT**: holds information, maintains the servers, surveils everything — the one who truly "sees all";
- **Mechanical**: holds infrastructure, maintains the generator and the entire silo's operation — the one "you cannot live without."

Each monopolizes an irreplaceable resource, and each distrusts the others, forming a fragile balance.

## 2. The Model: Veto-Player Coalitions

Let the three factions be $A, B, C$, each monopolizing a key resource. Any major decision requires at least two factions' support — because no single faction can simultaneously control "violence + information + infrastructure."

**Veto-player theory**: in a system requiring majority support, every faction is a "veto player." The system's stability depends on **no two factions having a motive to ally against the third.**

Let $V$ be the payoff of ruling alone, $V/2$ the payoff of a two-faction alliance (split equally), and $v_i$ each faction's payoff under the status quo. The alliance trigger condition is:

$$V/2 > v_i \quad \text{and} \quad V/2 > v_j$$

i.e., both factions judge that "splitting the third" beats their current situation.

## 3. Equilibrium Analysis: Which Two Factions Ally Easiest?

The key is the **substitutability** of the three resources:

- **Violence** (Judicial): can be replaced by "numbers + organization" — once residents awaken en masse, the monopoly on violence fails;
- **Information** (IT): can be replaced by "knowledge collaboration" — residents who record and reason independently can bypass the information monopoly;
- **Infrastructure** (Mechanical): **nearly irreplaceable** — without the generator, the elevators, the air circulation, no one survives.

So the most stable alliance is **Mechanical + anyone**: because Mechanical holds the key to "keeping the system alive," whoever allies with it gains the trump card to veto everything.

And the most dangerous combination is **Mechanical + IT**: infrastructure + information = the ability to keep everyone alive, kill everyone, and keep everyone from knowing what happened. Once this alliance forms, Judicial (violence) becomes redundant.

## 4. Show Verification

In the show, IT director Bernard is the nominal top power, but his true weakness is precisely Mechanical — because Mechanical controls the generator's maintenance, and the generator is the silo's lifeline. When Mechanical goes on strike, all of IT's surveillance and information advantage become meaningless: **you control the information, but you do not control the electricity that keeps the information system running.**

This is the embodiment of "infrastructure is irreplaceable." Any faction that looks powerful, but whose foundation rests in someone else's hands, has built its power on sand.

## 5. Falsifiable Corollary

> **In a closed system where "violence, information, and infrastructure" are separately monopolized, if the infrastructure faction (Mechanical) and the information faction (IT) form an alliance, a change of administration becomes inevitable, and the former violence monopolist (Judicial) is left in a passive, clearable position.**

The converse is falsifiable: if the three factions' resources are highly complementary, and no single faction holds irreplaceable infrastructure, the three-way checks can persist long-term.

## Conclusion

Power inside a closed system is never monolithic. Its stability is a **fragile equilibrium among three mutually distrustful factions** — an equilibrium that can be broken at any moment. And the key to breaking it lies not in information, not in violence, but in the humblest and most irreplaceable thing of all — **the infrastructure that keeps everything running.**
