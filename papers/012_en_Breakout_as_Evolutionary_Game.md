# Paper 12: Breakout as an Evolutionary Game and Migration

**Core game**: staying vs. breaking vs. migrating, replicator dynamics
**Tools**: evolutionary games, replicator dynamics, migration models, evolutionarily stable strategies
**Show anchor**: walking out, founding new silos, returning to the surface, outside survival
**Core thesis**: breakout is not a single event but the replacement process of an evolutionarily stable strategy
**Falsifiable corollary**: when outside survival probability exceeds inside survival probability, migration becomes the dominant strategy

---

## Abstract

This final paper of the series returns to the most fundamental question: **how does breakout actually happen?** The previous eleven papers argued for breakout's various mechanisms — signals, knowledge, collaboration, communication, experiments. This paper raises the perspective and uses evolutionary game theory to answer: breakout is not a single "a hero decides to leave" event, but the **replacement process of an evolutionarily stable strategy.** In a closed system, "staying" was once a stable survival strategy; but as external conditions change (truth assembled, collaboration networks formed, communication established), the payoff of "staying" keeps falling while the payoff of "migrating" keeps rising. When the two payoff curves cross, "migrating" replaces "staying" as the new evolutionarily stable strategy. Breakout is that moment.

## 1. The Scene: From Staying to Walking Out

The show's climax is not a single rebellion, but **the beginning of the exodus.** When Juliette, and more and more people, start choosing to "walk out" rather than "stay," the entire silo's game structure flips.

Before that moment, "staying" was rational (the outside is unknown; going out means death); after it, "walking out" became rational (the outside is known; staying is what means death).

## 2. The Model: Replicator Dynamics

Let a population hold three strategies:

- **Stay (S)**: remain in the silo, obey the rules, wait;
- **Break (B)**: try to break the rules, but stay inside the system;
- **Migrate (M)**: leave the system, walk into the outside.

**Replicator dynamics**: each strategy's proportion in the next generation is proportional to its **fitness** (payoff). Higher-payoff strategies get adopted by more people.

Let $u_S, u_B, u_M$ be the three strategies' payoffs. Crucially, these payoffs are **not fixed** — they change with the system's state:

- **Early**: $u_S > u_B > u_M$ (outside unknown, staying is safest) → most people stay;
- **Middle**: truth gradually assembled, $u_B$ rises, $u_S$ falls (staying starts to mean "waiting to die");
- **Late**: $u_M > u_S$ (outside survival probability verified higher than inside) → migration becomes the dominant strategy.

**Evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS)**: when $u_M$ stably exceeds $u_S$, "staying" is no longer evolutionarily stable — a small migrating group grows at higher fitness and eventually replaces the staying group.

## 3. Equilibrium Analysis: Breakout as a Phase Transition

Breakout is not a linear process, but a **phase transition.**

Before the critical point, "staying" looks unshakable — everyone obeys the rules because obeying truly is optimal. But the system's state keeps changing: truth is being assembled, collaboration networks expanding, communication being established, experiments accumulating. Every tiny change **quietly raises "migrating"'s payoff and lowers "staying"'s payoff.**

Until one day, the two payoff curves cross. At that moment, no "call to action" and no "hero" is needed — **the strategy's evolutionary dynamics alone will automatically push the whole population from "staying" toward "migrating."**

This is why every mechanism argued in the previous eleven papers (signals, knowledge, collaboration, communication, experiments) ultimately points to the same destination: **they are all changing the payoff curves, pushing the system toward that phase-transition point.**

## 4. Show Verification

In the show, Juliette's departure is not an isolated case — it is a **signal** marking that "migrating"'s payoff has exceeded "staying"'s. And after her, more and more people choose to leave, which is precisely the replicator dynamics at work: **one successful migrant demonstrates to everyone that "going out" is feasible and better.** Thus migration turns from one individual's risk into the population's direction.

## 5. Falsifiable Corollary

> **In a closed system, when the expected payoff of "outside survival" stably exceeds that of "inside staying," "migrating" necessarily replaces "staying" as the evolutionarily stable strategy, and the population's breakout becomes an irreversible trend — requiring no "hero" or "call," only the inevitable result of evolutionary dynamics.**

The converse is falsifiable: if the true probability of outside survival stayed permanently lower than inside staying (i.e., "the outside really kills"), staying could remain stable long-term. But the previous eleven papers already argued: in a system with knowledge, collaboration, communication, and experiments, the "truth" of outside survival probability will eventually be assembled — and once assembled, the phase transition is irreversible.

## Conclusion

The final picture of breakout is not a hero's victory, but the **rise and fall of a strategy.** A closed system is not "pushed down" — it is "evolved away." When "staying"'s payoff thins day by day and "migrating"'s payoff thickens day by day, the system's collapse is no longer a question of "whether" but "when."

And this entire series' conclusion can be compressed into one sentence:

> **No closed system can stop a basic fact — when staying inside becomes more dangerous than walking out, people will walk out.** And what makes "walking out" not dangerous is not courage, but four of humanity's oldest and most undefeatable things: **knowledge, collaboration, communication, and experimentation.**
