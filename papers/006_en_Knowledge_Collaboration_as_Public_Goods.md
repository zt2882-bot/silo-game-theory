# Paper 6: Knowledge Collaboration as a Public Goods Game

**Core game**: individual knowledge-sharing vs. free-riding, with network externalities
**Tools**: public goods games, network games, knowledge externalities, collaboration thresholds
**Show anchor**: porters passing information, mechanics recording, doctors observing, IT data
**Core thesis**: breakout needs no genius, only knowledge that can be recorded, transmitted, and collaborated on
**Falsifiable corollary**: once the collaboration network exceeds a threshold, breakout probability approaches 1

## Abstract

The most common imagination about "how to break a closed system" is "you need a genius." This paper argues that this imagination is wrong — and harmfully so: it mistakes a breakout's **accelerator** for its **engine**, thereby concealing the mechanism that actually does the work. The true necessary conditions for breaking a closed system are only three: knowledge can be recorded, transmitted, and collaborated on. A genius is an **accelerator, not an engine.** This paper models knowledge collaboration as a public goods game, arguing: when the collaboration network exceeds some size threshold, breakout probability approaches 1 — **even if the network contains no genius at all.**

## 1. The Scene: The Unremarkable People

The people actually doing "breakout work" in the silo are not some lone hero, but a group of unremarkable ordinary people:

- **Porters**: move goods between floors, and messages along with them;
- **Mechanics**: while repairing equipment, note the anomalies in pipe routes;
- **Doctors**: observe that a cleaner's time of death disagrees with the official account, and record it;
- **IT staff**: during routine maintenance, discover residues of deleted data.

Each holds only one fragment of the truth. But fragments assembled together become the whole truth.

## 2. The Model: Knowledge's Public-Good Nature

Knowledge is a classic **public good**:

- **Non-excludable**: one person's use does not prevent another's;
- **Non-rivalrous**: after knowledge is shared, the sharer loses nothing.

But public goods have a "free-rider" problem: since others will share, why should I take the risk of sharing? This creates the **startup problem** of knowledge collaboration — in a fearful closed system, the first sharer bears the highest risk with the most uncertain reward.

**The collaboration threshold theorem**: let the collaboration network's size be $n$, with each participant contributing one knowledge fragment. Breakout requires assembling $m$ fragments ($m$ fields of knowledge). When $n$ exceeds some threshold, the probability of assembling all $m$ fragments approaches 1:

$$P(\text{assembled}) = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{m} (1-p_i)^{n_i} \to 1 \quad \text{as } n_i \to \infty$$

The key is $n_i$: **the number of people in the network who can solve the $i$-th fragment.** The larger and more diverse the network, the higher the probability each fragment gets filled.

## 3. Equilibrium Analysis: Why Collaboration Is Inevitable

The free-rider problem in a closed system is outweighed by a stronger force: **the tension of being sealed.**

In an environment where information is systematically suppressed, "sharing knowledge" is itself an act of resistance. And knowledge collaboration has a **positive feedback**: each fragment added raises the whole network's credibility by one notch, attracting more people and bringing more fragments.

The administration can cut ties between individuals, but it cannot cut **the transmissibility of knowledge itself.** Once a small group starts collaborating, recording, and iterating, the blockade enters a countdown.

## 4. Show Verification

In the show, Juliette's recovery of the truth is almost a textbook demonstration of knowledge collaboration: she does not figure it out alone, but through a **relay** — the dispenser George left, the clues her mother left, the scraps held by different people on different floors, finally assembled into the complete truth.

**No one is a genius, but the collaboration network lets "ordinary people added together" approach a genius.**

## 5. Falsifiable Corollary

> **In a closed system with recording capability and collaboration conditions, once the number (and diversity) of people participating in knowledge collaboration exceeds a threshold, the probability that the knowledge needed for breakout is assembled within finite time approaches 1; this conclusion does not depend on the existence of any single "genius" individual.**

The converse is falsifiable: if the administration can simultaneously sever all recording, all transmission, and all collaboration, breakout can be prevented. But "severing all collaboration" means atomizing every individual completely — which itself consumes enormous resources and further intensifies the suppressed tension.

## Conclusion

Humanity's most powerful weapon was never some genius, but three plain facts: **knowledge can accumulate, can be taught, and can be collaborated on.** A genius only accelerates the process, not enables it. **What breaks a closed system is collaboration, not a hero.**
