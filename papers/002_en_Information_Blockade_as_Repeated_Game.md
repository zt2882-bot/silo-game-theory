# Paper 2: Information Blockade as a Repeated Game with Dissipative Structure

**Core game**: administration's blockade vs. residents' exploration, an infinitely repeated incomplete-information game
**Tools**: repeated games, Bayesian learning, knowledge as a public good, dissipative systems
**Show anchor**: history destruction, relic bans, the PEZ dispenser, notes and AI data
**Core thesis**: the cost of blockade rises over time, and the probability of breakout approaches 1
**Falsifiable corollary**: the stronger the blockade, the greater the internal tension, and the nearer the breakout threshold

---

## Abstract

The closed system's most central control instrument is information blockade: destroying history, banning relics, monopolizing knowledge. This paper argues that information blockade is, in game-theoretic terms, a **necessarily dissipative structure** — its maintenance cost rises monotonically over time, and the breakout probability it tries to suppress also rises monotonically over time. The two curves must cross at some finite moment. The conclusion: blockade can only delay breakout, never prevent it. And the price of delay is that the suppressed tension keeps accumulating inside the system, until it releases in a more violent form.

## 1. The Scene: Erased History

One of *Silo*'s defining settings is this: 140 years ago, after a rebellion, the residents destroyed their own history. Since then, any object concerning "the past" and "the outside" is taboo — relics are confiscated, speaking of "outside" is a crime, and even the very existence of "other silos" cannot be mentioned.

The administration's logic is direct: **as long as residents do not know what they are fighting, they cannot even ask the right question.**

## 2. The Model: Why Knowledge Cannot Be Sealed

Let $K$ be the knowledge asset (any information about the outside). The administration's strategy is to "erase every copy of $K$." But this strategy faces a structural obstacle:

**The Knowledge-Unsealability Theorem**: in any system with recording capability, once knowledge is created, it cannot be completely eliminated.

The argument is simple: knowledge exists in **multiple physical forms simultaneously** — paper documents, electronic data, AI training parameters, individual memory. To erase everything, the administration must control all storage media, all transmission channels, and all individual memories at once, and it must do so at the very moment the information is produced. This is equivalent to **complete information erasure**, whose cost grows **exponentially** with system complexity.

More fatally: even if erasure succeeds, the suppressed **tension** does not vanish. It persists in other forms — curiosity, suspicion, word-of-mouth fragments, and the next generation's independent reasoning from scratch.

## 3. Equilibrium Analysis: The Dissipative Structure

Treat the blockade as a dissipative system, and two monotonicities follow:

- **Maintenance cost $C(t)$ rises monotonically**: blockade demands continuous investment (surveillance, punishment, narrative upkeep, history rewriting), and the more complex the system and the more generations pass, the larger these investments.
- **Breakout probability $P(t)$ rises monotonically**: each generation performs independent Bayesian reasoning, deriving the sealed conclusion from scattered information; knowledge fragments accumulate across the collaboration network.

The two monotonic curves must cross at some finite moment $T$. Before $T$, the blockade looks effective; after $T$, breakout becomes an event with probability tending to 1.

**This is what dissipative structure means: a blockade is not a wall, but a dam that keeps leaking.** The harder you plug it, the higher the water rises, and the greater the impact when it bursts.

## 4. Show Verification

The show confirms this with an exquisitely precise prop: the **PEZ dispenser**.

Juliette recovers her memory not by "remembering," but by finding a dispenser left by George — a **physical carrier** holding information the administration had destroyed. That this dispenser survived 140 years of destruction itself proves: **erasing every copy is impossible in practice.**

More crucially, every "breakout" in the show is not a single genius's flash of insight, but **the reassembly of knowledge fragments** — one person's observed anomaly, plus another's record, plus a third's inference. This confirms the model's second monotonicity: breakout probability rises as knowledge fragments accumulate.

## 5. Falsifiable Corollary

> **In any closed system with recording capability, if the administration's blockade intensity (investment in surveillance, punishment, and destruction) rises monotonically over time, then the system's internal counter-tension also rises monotonically, and the breakout threshold necessarily arrives within finite time.**

The converse is falsifiable: if a closed system existed whose blockade intensity rose while its breakout probability stayed at zero over the long run, the core thesis would be refuted. **In human civilization's history, we have not yet found such a counterexample.**

## Conclusion

The tragedy of information blockade is that **the harder it tries, the more it fails.** Blockade does not produce obedience; it produces tension. Tension is not eliminated — it is stored. And stored tension eventually releases in a more violent form.
